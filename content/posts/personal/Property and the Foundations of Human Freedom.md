Most people think of freedom in terms of speech, assembly, or the right to vote. In _Property and Freedom_[^1], Richard Pipes argues that one quiet requirement has supported every free society across history. That requirement is the right to own private property. When individuals cannot own the things around them, political and civil liberties cannot last. Property rights draw an essential boundary between state power and personal independence.

Pipes first separates simple possession from true property, to show why property matters. Possession means having physical control over something, which happens everywhere in nature. Birds guard nesting territories to feed their young, male deer fight over grazing grounds, and plants compete for sunlight and soil.

The property is a legal concept, while possession is simply a natural instinct. As Aristotle pointed out thousands of years ago, something becomes true property only when a person has the legal right to sell, trade, or transfer it without arbitrary interference. Early civilizations in Mesopotamia and Egypt treated all land as the personal estate of kings or temples. Ancient Israel took a sharply different path by placing divine limits on royal power and treating property as a sacred boundary.

The biblical foundation for property rests directly on moral law and early civil codes. The commandment against theft recognizes that individuals hold a right to personal belongings that others, including rulers, must respect. Biblical law also forbade moving the boundary stones that marked family land plots, treating tampering with land markers as a serious moral offense. In addition, biblical land ownership stayed tied to families through safeguards like the Jubilee year. Under this rule, land sold during hard times returned to its original family line every fifty years, which prevented permanent landlessness and kept total wealth from concentrating in the hands of a ruling elite.

The biblical story of King Ahab and Naboth's vineyard shows this limit on royal power in action. When King Ahab wanted Naboth's land to expand his palace grounds, he offered to buy it or trade it for another plot. Naboth refused because the land was his family inheritance. Even as king, Ahab could not simply seize the vineyard by royal decree, because the monarch remained bound by divine law protecting private ownership. The land was taken only after Queen Jezebel arranged a criminal conspiracy built on false accusations and murder, an act that brought swift condemnation upon the royal house. The story made clear that private ownership stood above the arbitrary desires of the ruler.

Centuries later, ancient Rome built on these ideas by drawing a clear line in its legal codes, which the philosopher Seneca summed up as monarchs rule, while subjects own. Comparing the histories of England and Russia shows just how much that distinction matters.

In England, private land ownership took root early. When medieval kings ran out of personal wealth to pay for wars and government, they could not simply take what they wanted. They had to call a parliament of private landowners to ask for tax money. Each time the king needed funds, parliament demanded stronger rights, legal protections, and political influence in return. Over the centuries, that bargaining produced parliamentary democracy, the rule of law, and modern civil liberties. People won their freedom because the state had to ask permission before taking private wealth.

In Russia, history moved in the opposite direction. After centuries of Mongol rule, Russian Tsars adopted a system where the ruler owned and governed everything at once. In that patrimonial state, independent private property did not exist. The Tsar owned the land, the industry, and ultimately the lives of the people. Because the ruler already owned everything, he had no reason to negotiate with subjects or build a representative parliament to raise taxes. Rulers like Peter the Great simply calculated their expenses and forced the population to pay. Without private property to act as a shield against state power, Russia never developed a true rule of law, an independent middle class, or basic civil rights. The sovereign kept total authority to seize assets, exile people, or execute anyone at will.

When totalitarian regimes in the twentieth century abolished private property, the mass loss of life that followed was predictable. Stripping people of property takes away their ability to survive on their own, forcing them to depend entirely on the state for food, shelter, and daily existence. When the state controls everything, it controls everyone. Protecting private property preserves personal wealth, and even more importantly, it serves as the ultimate shield for human dignity and keeps free societies free.
