I often think of intriguing poem[^1] by Rumi. Reading it through a Hasidic lens feels like walking into a familiar study hall. The central themes of human choice, divine presence, and the quiet surrender of the ego sit at the very heart of Hasidic life.

Looking at the opening reflection on human action and divine action immediately recognizes the classic tension between the truth that there is nothing aside from God and human free will.[^2] In daily life, we experience ourselves as independent authors of our choices. Yet Hasidic philosophy teaches that every ounce of energy we use to move a hand, speak a word, or feel an emotion comes directly from the Creator at that exact second. The poem captures this mystery vividly through the image of the two trembling hands. One shakes from illness, and the other shakes from the momentum of a strike. Both movements rely entirely on divine life force, yet the human soul carries moral weight for the strike. From a Hasidic standpoint, this is the great design of creation. God steps back, hiding His absolute control behind the veil of nature and psychology, specifically so that we can feel like separate beings capable of genuine choice.

This brings us to the contrast between blaming fate and taking responsibility. When we stumble, the mind easily reaches for excuses, pointing out that God orchestrated the circumstances or created our desires. Teachings on _Teshuvah_ (return to God) emphasize that true spiritual maturity begins when we drop that defense. Even though the Creator knows every outcome from the beginning to the end of time, we live inside the present moment, where our reverence and accountability matter completely. Taking ownership of our missteps opens the door to real intimacy with the Divine, because we treat our relationship with God as real and consequential.

The poem also speaks to the limits of the analytical mind. Like the scientist who can solve every physical puzzle yet misses the realm of wonder, human intellect has a ceiling. Hasidic thought places immense value on deep study and contemplation, using the mind to refine the heart. At the same time, it teaches that the deepest truths of faith live above logic. From our limited vantage point inside time, free will and total divine control look like a contradiction. From the perspective of the Infinite, they exist together seamlessly. A Hasid learns to hold both truths at once, acting with full personal responsibility while knowing that everything rests in God's open hand.

Perhaps the most resonant part of the poem is its quiet arrival at emptiness, or self-nullification. It is the realization that our loud, anxious sense of self is like a sunbeam inside the sun. When a ray of light stays inside the sun, it has no separate identity at all, but it is completely absorbed in its source. Similarly, when we let go of our preoccupation with our own importance, our grievances, and our isolation, we discover a paradoxical fullness.

In Hasidic life, however, reaching this state serves a clear purpose. Becoming empty of ego clears space for God's presence to shine through our daily lives. When we stop being so tightly bound up in our own narrow self-interest, we become genuinely present for the people around us. Emptiness becomes a clean vessel, ready to carry kindness, perform a commandment, and bring a quiet spark of holiness into the physical world.

    Consider the difference
    in our actions and God’s actions.
    
    We often ask, “Why did you do that?”
    or “Why did I act like that?”
    
    We do act, and yet everything we do
    is God’s creative action.
    
    We look back and analyze the events
    of our lives, but there is another way
    of seeing, a backward-and-forward-at-once
    vision, that is not rationally understandable.
    
    Only God can understand it.
    Satan made the excuse, You caused me to fall,
    whereas Adam said to God, We did this
    to ourselves. After this repentance,
    God asked Adam, Since all is within
    my foreknowledge, why didn’t you
    defend yourself with that reason?
    
    Adam answered, I was afraid,
    and I wanted to be reverent.
    
    Whoever acts with respect will get respect.
    Whoever brings sweetness will be served almond cake.
    Good women are drawn to be with good men.
    
    Honor your friend.
    Or treat him rudely,
    and see what happens!
    
    Love, tell an incident now
    that will clarify this mystery
    of how we act freely, and are yet
    compelled. One hand shakes with palsy.
    Another shakes because you slapped it away.
    
    Both tremblings come from God,
    but you feel guilty for the one,
    and what about the other?
    
    These are intellectual questions.
    The spirit approaches the matter
    differently. Omar once had a friend, a scientist,
    Bu’l-Hakam, who was flawless at solving
    empirical problems, but he could not follow Omar
    into the area of illumination and wonder.
    
    Now I return to the text, “And He is with you, wherever you are,” but when have I ever left it!
    
    Ignorance is God’s prison.

    Knowing is God’s palace.
    
    We sleep in God’s unconsciousness.

    We wake in God’s open hand.
    
    We weep God’s rain.

    We laugh God’s lightning.
    
    Fighting and peacefulness

    both take place within God.
    
    Who are we then in this complicated world-tangle,
    that is really just the single, straight line down at the beginning of ALLAH?
    
    Nothing.

    We are emptiness.
    
    When you are with everyone but me, you’re with no one.

    When you are with no one but me, you’re with everyone.

    Instead of being so bound up with everyone, be everyone.

    When you become that many, you’re nothing.

    Empty.

     _The Essential Rumi, New Expanded Edition. tr. Coleman Barks_
